Central to the Nation of Islam's mission was not only spiritual transformation
but also sartorial transformation. Dress and appearance were strictly
regulated, with members adhering to modest, uniform styles that communicated
discipline, respectability, and order. Through this carefully curated
aesthetic, the NOI projected an image of strength, moral clarity, and
resistance to mainstream cultural norms. Indeed, fashion became a key part of
how the NOI constructed and expressed its values, including those concerned
with masculinity and femininity.

Fashion was reflected in the visuals that were included in Muhammad Speaks,
the NOI's newspaper. In this example dating to 1971, the popularity of platform
shoes, which had become essential to Soul Style, disco culture, and broader
Black urban fashion in the early 1970s, is parodied through a three-panel
sequence.

The first two panels introduce a domestic scene. A husband appears to
surprise his wife with a pair of platform shoes, a gift meant to express
care and indulgence. The wife seems ecstatic as she dons the new items,
declaring that she can't wait to flaunt them in public. The scene appears
to be a harmless conjunction of shared passion for consumer fashion and
marital affection. However, the comic takes a sharp turn as she encounters a
flamboyantly dressed man, most likely her husband, who also wears oversized
platform shoes. He points at her feet while shouting: "Woman, get outa my
shoes!"

Echoing Elijah Muhammad's messages, this cartoon suggests that white, Western
clothing styles have collapsed the boundaries between male and female. By
aligning the woman with the effeminate man, this cartoon mocks both: the woman
for embracing decadent fashion, and the man for embodying the gender confusion
the NOI viewed as evidence of moral decline.

This moment reflects the broader critique described by Zareena Grewal, who
argues that the NOI saw Black participation in secular American culture as
a trap that produced spiritual and social degradation. Additionally, in this
image and elsewhere, popular Black styles were frequently targeted by the NOI
as indicators of assimilation into white-controlled consumer culture.

Through this humorous triptych, the cartoon reinforces Elijah Muhammad's
claim that proper dress is central to civilization, self-respect, and divine
order. Visual satire thus functions as a theological device. By ridiculing
platform shoes and their broader aesthetic and cultural world, this comic strip
urges readers to reject 1970s fashion trends and instead embrace the modest,
disciplined, and gender-specific attire that defines the Nation of Islam's
ideal of the civilized Black Muslim male and female subject.
