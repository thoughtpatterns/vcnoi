In the summer of 1963, the world witnessed a Black freedom struggle
as never before. Televised scenes and front-page photos of police dogs
in Birmingham, the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church, and the
assassination of Medgar Evers, narrated a visual contrast of Southern
white violence and peaceful Black protest.

By design, nonviolent activism was meant to provoke confrontations
with white power, which would stir public outrage and compel federal
intervention. Yet in Black communities, the wisdom of nonviolence was
hotly debated. On a national stage Malcolm X had rejected the philosophy
of nonviolence, exposing what he described as the hypocrisy of white
American violence and admonishing the naivete of Black leaders.

Muhammad Speaks regularly published commentary that was critical of
nonviolent activism. But it was Eugene Majied's political cartoons that
stood out.  His visual argument aimed to expose contradictions and pitfalls
in the nonviolence movement. His images also provide a window into the
Nation of Islam's worldview, which shaped its critique of nonviolence.

This illustration by Eugene Majied is titled "But Never a Word About
Self-Defense!" Here, a Black woman lays on her back, with an agonized
expression, bleeding profusely from her head and mouth, as two white
police officers with menacing smiles press down on her: one with his knee
on her neck and the other restraining her wrist and pressing her torso
as he forcefully raises her leg skyward. With bruised eyes she looks
to her right towards "Rev. M. L. King," shown crouching on his hands
and knees while holding a bible. King offers no assistance, except to
pointedly admonish the victim, saying "If there is any blood spilled in
the streets, let it be our blood!" Behind them, other officers assault
Black women, with one officer dangling a woman by her ankles as other
white men violently tear the clothing off the bruised, bleeding and
weeping bodies of Black women detained in a patrol wagon. In the furthest
background, a courthouse bears the motto "Liberty and Justice for All,"
while an American flag waves above.

Majied's image aims to undermine the moral high ground of nonviolence,
depicting it as a submission to white violence. From the vantage point
of the Nation of Islam, it is also a failure to protect Black women. A
core tenet of the Nation of Islam's religious framework requires Muslim
men to physically protect women's dignity and safety, by any means
necessary. In her analysis of Malcolm X's public discourse, historian
Farah Jasmine Griffin described this as the NOI's "price of protection"
through which Black women receive a sense of safety previously reserved
for white women and in exchange, Black men attain a sense of possession
previously reserved only for white men. Majied's choice to depict the
physical and possible sexual violation of Black women likely struck
Muslim readers as a failure of religious obligation. It also evoked a
broader sense of moral failure to defend one's people.

The background imagery is significant. The purported American ideals
of justice and equality stand in stark contrast to the institutional
and interpersonal racism that has shaped American life. King's words
further highlight this contraction, demonstrating the hypocrisy of
whites demanding nonviolence from Black protesters while endorsing
institutionalized white violence.

As a motif, hypocrisy has a religious significance in Majied's oeuvre. In
many of his drawings, Majied seeks to expose the moral and spiritual
corruption of 'hypocrites' who outwardly display a value they inwardly
deny or refuse to act upon. For instance, one of his popular drawings
depicts "Uncle Sam" as a "Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde," split in two,
failing to live up to his moral claims.

In many Islamic communities, including the Nation of Islam, hypocrisy
represents a moral degradation. In place of King's philosophy, Majied
highlights the Islamic ideal of actively resisting hypocrisy. For the
NOI, standing firm against hypocrites is essential for spiritual and
political liberation, even if King and others fail to do so.
