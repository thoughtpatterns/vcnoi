The NOI and the U.S. Penal System's fraught relationship was forged by the
1942 arrest and imprisonment of Elijah Muhammad, leader of the Nation of Islam,
and the thirty Muslims who protested his arrest. His incarceration also marked
some of the first prison conversions, a method of recruiting that would go on to
create an association between Black and Muslim identities behind bars.

As the scholar Edward Curtis IV notes, the NOI viewed the Black Muslim body
as the literal "site of struggle" between a system of white supremacy and the
freedom ensured by Islam; testimonies about which often included in a section
entitled "What Islam Has Done For Me" in the NOI's newspaper, Muhammad Speaks.

Graphic artists working for Muhammad Speaks also developed a bold pictorial
language capable of identifying and connecting the different perpetrators
of oppression --- such as the police, courts, prisons, and laws --- to
visually argue that the US criminal-legal system's primary purpose was not
the enforcement of justice, but the continuation of black subjugation. These
graphics insist that the crisis of mass incarceration goes beyond the
jails. Instead, it is a direct attack on Black freedom, dignity, and manhood
that only enlightenment and empowerment through Islam can counter.

Among many examples, in the section of "Prison News in Black," Brother Wendell X
Farris recounts his wrongful conviction, life inside the "house of correction,"
and gratitude to Allah for guiding the jury to recognize his innocence. A
graphic accompanying his first-person account depicts a figure seen from
behind, staring through prison bars. The prisoner's head takes the shape of a
lightbulb that has been turned on by NOI teachings, represented here by its
star-and-crescent moon logo. The flag also refers to the NOI's nationhood,
revealing: through conversion, many found not only freedom, but also a deep
sense of "brotherhood, that made them feel loved, and helped them succeed in
life".

In this graphic, the imprisoned man's spiritual enlightenment emits rays of
light that appear as if breaking through the bars, creating a halo of sorts
around him. Jail constraints are thus reinterpreted as opportunities to practice
physical and mental discipline, which become a path to freedom itself. In other
words, the image offers visual testimony asserting that conversion to Islam is
the only path to restore Black masculinity, dignity, and community for those
targeted by the United States' white supremacist legal system.

By reframing confinement as an opportunity for moral and bodily transformation
through Islam, the NOI insists that even the most controlled and surveilled
spaces cannot fully extinguish the Black spirit. Where the prison is meant
to degrade, Islam restores; where the U.S. justice system denies dignity,
Elijah Muhammad promises a path to reclaim it. By positioning Islam as the
only force capable of reversing that degradation, the newspaper Muhammad Speaks
and its illustrations reframe imprisonment as a site of possibility where the
disciplined Black Muslim body can be remade into a symbol of sovereignty rather
than subjugation.
